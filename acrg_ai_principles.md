# ACRG Principles for the Use of Agentic AI

**Version 0.2 (draft for group discussion), October 2026**

## Why we have these

Agentic AI can now help with almost all of our modelling and data analysis, and the norms around it are still unsettled. We are publicly funded to understand climate change and ozone depletion, so if these tools can improve our science we should take them seriously. Their benefits are hard to measure, though, even for the people using them, so we try not to take them for granted.

The university, funders and journals have their own AI policies, and where they apply, they come first. They mostly cover what we declare when we publish, and say much less about how we work day to day. These principles are our shared view on that part.

## Principles

**1. Use agentic AI to do more ambitious and robust science.**
These tools may open up questions that were out of reach, and ignoring them has a cost. When they save us time, we'd rather spend it on rigour than on simply producing more. The group will make time for this and provide shared tools and access. Most technical training will come from the university, but we'll share our own experiences and what has worked for us.

**2. Be transparent about your AI use.**
Saying how you used AI should be routine and nothing to feel awkward about. The practical guidelines suggest simple labels for this. When you publish, follow the venue's disclosure rules.

**3. Make sure you can defend what you and the agent have produced.**
Work is yours when you can stand behind every claim in it without the tool, however it was made. The mistakes are yours too, as is crediting other people's work and data, whoever brought it in. Some of us hold stricter standards for our own writing or code, including not using AI at all, and that's fine.

**4. Protect the skills that matter to you.**
AI use can stop us developing skills, or diminish ones we have. This applies to everyone, whatever their experience, and the skills that matter will differ from person to person. With other group members, think about which skills matter most and how to keep practising them, and every so often check you can still perform key tasks without the tool.

**5. Use it with open eyes.**
This technology has real costs, including its environmental footprint, the concentration of power in a few companies, disputed training data, and the race to build ever more capable systems. These costs are part of why we leave much of the choice to you. We ask everyone to learn the tools well enough to make an informed choice and to review colleagues' AI-assisted work. Beyond that, how much you rely on them is up to you, and the group won't hold it against you if you use them sparingly for ethical reasons. What we can reasonably expect of each other will change if these tools become central to our science, so we'll keep revisiting this, and keep asking what would make us stop, switch, or step back.

We review these principles together each term. The practical guidelines and our shared tools put them into practice.
