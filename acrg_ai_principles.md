# ACRG Principles for the Use of Agentic AI

**Version 0.1 (draft for group discussion) — August 2026**

## Motivation

Agentic AI is applicable to essentially all of our modelling and data-analysis workflows. The question is no longer where these tools can be used, but how their use changes accountability, learning, and independence. The technology is here to stay, but the norms around it are not yet settled.

We are publicly funded to advance understanding of climate change and ozone depletion. If tools exist that can make our science better, we are obliged to take them seriously. However, because of the potential risks to our integrity, the quality of our work, and our long-term skills development, we are equally obliged to demonstrate, rather than assume, that they can improve our outputs. 

Institutional, funder, and journal policies govern what we declare at the point of publication. They say little about how we work in the months before it. That is the gap these principles aim to fill.  Nevertheless, the Atmospheric Chemistry Research Group is part of the University of Bristol. Nothing in this document can overrides University policy. Where the two conflict, University policy must take precedence.

## Principles

1. **These tools have the potential to expand the questions we can realistically ask. The group commits to genuinely exploring them to pursue more ambitious science.** However, we do not assume that the tools are inherently beneficial. 

2. **Engagement is supported, not assumed.** Recognising that fluency is becoming a professional skill like statistics or version control, the group provides time and peer-to-peer support.

3. **Accountability stays human.** You own the errors in AI-assisted work; you therefore cannot also claim the tool's fluency as your insight. You are responsible for ensuring proper attribution of others' work and data, whether you or an agent introduced it. Where AI accelerates our use of others' data, our attribution and engagement with providers keeps pace.

4. **Ownership means defensibility.** Work is yours when every claim in it is one you assert and can defend without the tool, whatever helped you get there. This is a standard on the product and your understanding of it, not on the process. Members may hold stricter personal standards on AI in their own writing or code, including none at all; the group treats this spread as healthy.

5. **Disclosure is cheap and stigma-free.** "The agent wrote the pipeline; I verified it against tests I trust" should be an unremarkable sentence in this group.

6. **Verification is the scarce skill.** We celebrate judgement: questions well chosen, errors caught, decisions defended. Output volume is not a measure of success. 

7. **Protect learning.** Evidence shows AI assistance can impair skill acquisition even while it boosts short-term output. We aim to use the tools in a way that maintains critical scientific thinking and practises the skills the tools bypass: deep reading, derivation, debugging, writing as thinking.

8. **Protect independence.** We keep some deliberately AI-free spaces (journal club, whiteboard sessions) so that our priors form independently before they are shared — or correlated.

9. **Protect conscience.** Members may hold sincere ethical objections to this industry. Non-use on such grounds is respected.

10. **Open-eyed compromise.** We use these tools while acknowledging unresolved harms in how they are made and the industry that makes them: environmental costs, concentration of power, contested training-data provenance, and risks from the race to build ever more capable systems. We do not pretend this compromise is fully right.

These principles are a living document, reviewed termly by the whole group. They are implemented through our practical guidelines and our shared tooling, which change more often than the principles do.
